Episode 16

When “He’s Still My Dad” Means Two Different Things

There is a sentence families say when they don’t know what else to say. “Well — he’s still your dad.” Sometimes it’s comfort. Sometimes it’s a shutdown. Sometimes, if you’re honest, it’s both in the same breath.

Anyone who has loved a complicated parent knows the split-screen feeling. On one side: the birthday calls, the advice that actually landed, the version of them that showed up and made you feel like you could do anything. On the other: the things you don’t say at the dinner table. The apologies that never came. The years you spent managing what they wouldn’t face.

Most of us were taught, quietly or loudly, to pick a screen and stay on it. Either they’re a good parent and you don’t talk about the rest, or they’re a bad parent and you don’t mention the good. Families enforce this like a rule. “Don’t speak ill.” “Honor them.” “Let it go.” And so the story gets edited down to whichever version keeps the peace — while the unedited version keeps you up at night.

Here’s what nobody tells you: you don’t have to pick. More than one thing can be true at the same time. A person can be the one who believed in you most and the one who hurt you deepest. Those aren’t contradictions to resolve. They’re the full picture, and healing only starts when you stop cropping it.

The Phone Call That Went Left

Every family has one — the conversation that started honest and ended in wreckage. Someone finally said the true thing out loud, and someone else heard it as an attack, and suddenly nobody was talking about the actual subject anymore. They were talking about loyalty. About who gets to tell the story. About whether loving someone means agreeing with them.

Here’s the reframe that changes everything: you can love someone and respect them and still disagree about what happened. The world is not made of yes-men and yes-women. “It’s not what you say, it’s how you say it” — and even more than that, it’s whether you can listen to a perspective that threatens yours without diminishing the person holding it.

When that first raw conversation goes left, it isn’t proof you shouldn’t have it. It’s proof you weren’t ready yet — not mature enough in the healing, not at the same place. Grace, real grace, means allowing the first draft to be messy. Baby language has tantrums. That doesn’t mean you stop talking. It means you give each other room to grow into the conversation.

Grace With Boundaries

There’s a version of grace that most of us learned, and it’s incomplete. It sounds like: “People make mistakes. It was a tough situation. He shouldn’t have done that, but—” And then the “but” quietly cancels everything that came before it.

Real grace holds two things that feel opposite: compassion for the person’s wounds AND a clear-eyed boundary about their behavior. Hurt people hurt people — that’s an explanation, not an excuse. You can understand that someone was carrying unprocessed trauma from their own childhood and still say: what you did was wrong, and I won’t be around it.

This is the sentence that sets people free: you can love somebody and you can leave them. You can love your parent and need them out of your home. You can honor what they gave you and refuse what they cost you. Love without boundaries isn’t love — it’s self-abandonment with a nicer name. And boundaries without love is just a wall. Healing asks for both, held at the same time.

Tell the Whole Story

Families love an edited narrative. The comfortable version gets retold; the rest gets filed under “we don’t talk about that.” But here’s what the edited version costs: the people living the unedited reality stay alone in it.

When you cut parts of the story out, you don’t protect anyone — you abandon the people still inside the hard parts. The kid in foster care. The spouse doing the math on whether to stay. The sibling who can’t reconcile the parent they knew with the parent you describe. They need the whole story, because the whole story is the only thing that actually helps.

And nobody gets to veto your telling because the person in the story is gone. “It’s not fair for you to tell me I can’t tell my story because a person who’s in it is not here.” Your story is yours. The people who love you will make room for all of it — the gratitude and the grief, the love and the limit.

Bottom Line

You don’t have to choose between loving your parent and telling the truth about them. Hold both. Give grace and set boundaries. And tell the whole story — because the unedited version is the one that sets everybody free.
