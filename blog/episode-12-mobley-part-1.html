Episode 12 · Guest: Dr. Phillip J. Mobley

The Man Who Became My Father

In 1988, a teenager living with foster parents walked into a small church and met a Sunday school teacher named Phil Mobley. What made the relationship take root wasn't a program or a formal intervention — it was a single, repeated question. Mobley would simply ask him how things were going, and mean it. For a kid who'd learned not to trust adults with the truth, that small, consistent gesture became the first crack in a wall that had been up for years.

He's candid about what that question actually did. He'd stopped expecting anyone outside his immediate circle to wonder how he was sleeping at night, let alone ask directly. The fact that someone kept asking, without an agenda attached to the answer, reshaped how he understood his own worth.

Rich Poor Kids and the Debt of Being Helped

Mobley traces his own drive to help others back to his childhood in rural Columbia County, one of six kids in a household without running water or electricity until he was five. He describes himself and his siblings as "the rich poor kids" — financially struggling but rich in family stability, faith, and community. What shaped him most, he says, wasn't the hardship itself but the adults who noticed him along the way and invested in him anyway.

That debt, as Mobley frames it, came with an obligation attached. Watching people invest in him without expecting anything back convinced him he owed the same thing forward, eventually leading him away from a successful corporate technology career and toward a life built around mentoring young people, particularly African American boys navigating instability at home.

Availability Over Capability

With so many kids who could use help, how do you know which ones are worth pouring extra time into? Mobley credits a mentor of his own with the answer that's guided his approach for decades — it's not your capability, it's your availability. Rather than scouting for the "right" kids to save, Mobley made himself consistently present and let the relationships that needed to form find their own way to him.

He's candid that this approach meant embracing things he didn't personally understand, including his mentee's teenage rap performances, which he admits he often couldn't follow and didn't always enjoy. He listened anyway, because the point was never the music — it was showing up for the person making it, without an agenda to fix or reshape anyone into something more comfortable for the adult in the room.

A Family, Not Just a Program

What began as weekend visits eventually became something more permanent. When his mentee turned 18 during senior year and technically aged out of the foster system, the Mobleys didn't treat it as a decision requiring debate — the entire household treated it as settled, and it meant avoiding a shelter placement he'd been threatened with for months.

That decision meant a mentorship relationship eventually became family in every sense that mattered, including a trip years later when the Mobleys rented a van and drove across the country just to spend Christmas together after he'd married and moved away.

Bottom Line

The goal was never recognition or even certainty of a positive outcome, since opening yourself up to help young people means accepting disappointment along the way. What matters is staying available long enough to find the relationships worth the investment, and trusting that whatever blessing comes from it may not arrive right away — but it does arrive, later, in ways that outlast the original gesture by decades.
