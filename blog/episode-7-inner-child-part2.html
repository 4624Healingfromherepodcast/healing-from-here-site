Episode 7

Your Inner Child Is Making Adult Decisions

It's easy to assume that once childhood ends, its influence ends with it. But a lot of what looks like an adult reaction — a sudden wave of anger, an overwhelming need for reassurance, a decision made from fear rather than clarity — is often a much younger version of that person still steering from the background.

Emotional Memory Isn't the Same as Recall

A person doesn't need to consciously remember a specific childhood event for it to still shape their reactions today. Emotional memory works differently than factual memory — it stores the feeling of an experience even when the details fade, and that feeling can resurface fully intact the moment something in the present resembles it. This is why a seemingly small trigger can produce a reaction that feels enormous: the nervous system isn't just responding to today. It's responding to every similar moment stacked underneath it.

Understanding this reframes overreaction. It's rarely about the present moment being that serious. It's about an old, unresolved feeling finally finding an opening to surface.

Forgiveness Without Forgetting

One of the more common traps in healing is the belief that forgiveness requires forgetting, or that remembering what happened means forgiveness hasn't truly occurred. Neither is true. It's possible to fully remember an experience, understand exactly how it shaped you, and still release the grip it has over your present decisions. Forgiveness isn't erasure — it's a shift in what power the memory is allowed to hold.

Getting stuck on "I forgive but I don't forget" as a reason to stay guarded misses this distinction. The goal was never forgetting. The goal is no longer being controlled by what's remembered.

You're Not That Little Kid Anymore

Perhaps the most freeing realization in this kind of work is also the simplest: the person reacting today is not the same person who experienced the original wound. They have more resources, more perspective, and more choice available to them now than they did as a child with no power over their circumstances. The inner child's fear made sense at the time it was formed. It doesn't have to keep making the decisions now.

Villainizing the people who caused early harm can feel justified, but it can also become its own trap — a way of staying tethered to the past instead of stepping fully into the freedom available in the present.

Bottom Line

The inner child is not in control unless it's allowed to be. Recognizing when an old wound — not the present moment — is driving a reaction is the first step toward finally letting the adult in the room make the decision instead.
