Episode 17

The Birthday That Changes Everything

For most young people, turning 18 brings a mix of excitement and nerves. There may be a graduation party, a dorm room to furnish, or parents who keep the bedroom ready in case things don't work out. For a young person in foster care, that same birthday can mean packing everything into a plastic bag and walking out the door with no address waiting on the other side.

The system calls it "aging out." The phrase sounds tidy, almost administrative. In practice, it often means a teenager loses housing, adult support, and any safety net in a single day, sometimes before finishing high school. Some feel relief, as if a sentence has ended. Others feel terror. Many feel both at once, and they carry that mix into adulthood with little help sorting it out.

The Weight of a Label

Children in care learn early that a single word can shape how people treat them. "Foster kid" may look like a category on paper, but to the child it often lands as "less than." It can feel like being the generic brand on the shelf: accepted, but never chosen. When adults receive a check for a child's care, some children sense that their worth is being measured in dollars, and that lesson is hard to unlearn.

The opposite is just as powerful. One caregiver who says, "You are not my foster child, you are my child," can give a young person something no program can fund: a sense of belonging. Belonging tells a child they are somebody, not a case number. Adults who care for these young people should pay close attention to their words. Labels can wound, and they can also heal.

Where Do You Go When There's No Home?

The effects of growing up in care don't stop at 18. Holidays, school breaks, and milestones can stir up the same question again and again: where do I belong? A young adult may have family in the same city and still feel there's no place they're truly welcome. That sense of displacement can follow someone into the military, college, a first job, and even their own marriage.

When young people leave care with no stable place to land, they become easy targets. People who know someone needs food or shelter can take advantage of that need. Telling these young adults to pull themselves up by their bootstraps ignores a simple truth: it's hard to plan a career when you're trying to survive the night. What helps is practical: transitional housing tied to work or school, mentors who stay, counseling to process years of hurt, and exposure to real career paths. Some fields, real estate among them, allow people to get licensed at 18 in many states. Young people can't pursue what nobody tells them exists.

Families have a role too. A parent who couldn't keep a child at home may still show up now. Presence matters more than a payment. A parent who only mails support from a distance offers something, but children need people in the room: people who correct them, cheer for them, and stay.

Bottom Line

Too many stories about foster care end at the moment things went wrong: the removal, the court date, the eighteenth birthday. Healing asks a different question: how can we help from here? That question applies to communities, to churches, to donors who could fund housing, and to the relatives who can still reach out today.

To any young person still in the system: this season is not the whole story. You will grow up, and your life will become yours to build. Let go of what you can, get counseling, find people who see you clearly, and keep moving. Your past may explain where you started, but it does not decide where you finish. You were aged out, not counted out, and you matter to this world.
