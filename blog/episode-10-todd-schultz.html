Episode 10 · Guest: Dr. Todd Schultz

The Cost of Telling the Truth: What a Psychologist's Story Teaches Us About Family, Healing, and Freedom

Most families have a myth. A story they tell themselves — and each other — about who they are, how great things are, and how much everyone is thriving. And somewhere in that family, there's usually at least one person who knows it isn't true.

Todd's Story

Todd spent his career writing about other people — studying their lives, their personalities, their emotional histories. But a few years ago, he did something he had never done before: he wrote about himself. Specifically, he wrote a deeply personal essay about his father's narcissism, what it was like growing up in a family where everyone was enlisted to protect a delusion, and how that shaped — and cost — him throughout his life.

The essay was rewritten four times. His editor pushed him to build the entire piece around his father, not just use him as an example. Once Todd committed to that, something unlocked. The story flowed. And when it was published, readers responded with one word: vulnerable. That surprised Todd. He wasn't trying to be vulnerable. He was just trying to tell the truth.

The Family Myth

In Todd's household, the strategy was to lie. To pretend everything was perfect. Every family member was enrolled in the program of protecting the patriarch's image — a man whose brittle narcissism required constant reinforcement from everyone around him.

When Todd began to see through the myth as a child, he became the target. As he described it, it's like leaving a cult — the cult doesn't just let you walk away. It goes after you. It tries to destroy you. This is the cost of telling the truth inside a family system built on delusion. And yet, Todd says, staying in that delusion is even more costly.

"A Slow Motion Suicide"

When asked what happens to people who choose to stay silent — to keep protecting the family myth rather than risk the fallout — Todd didn't mince words. He called it a slow motion suicide. Existentially. You know you're living a lie. And that knowledge, over time, hollows you out.

He acknowledged it's the easier path in the short term. You don't make waves. You don't lose the family gathering invitations. But the cost accumulates. Because keeping the peace, in that context, means keeping yourself hostage.

Attachment Theory and the Programs We Inherit

Todd also broke down attachment theory in a way that was both accessible and profound. From the very beginning of our lives, we start learning what works to make us feel safe with our primary caregivers. Those strategies get refined, internalized, and eventually become something like a computer program — automatically activated in every relationship we encounter as adults.

Only about 52% of the population is what researchers call securely attached. The other 48% are either anxiously insecure — constantly fearing abandonment, desperately seeking love — or avoidant, having learned that closeness leads to pain. These patterns, rooted in early childhood, follow us until we choose to examine and challenge them. The good news: they can change. Not easily. Not quickly. But they can.

Bottom Line

The cost of telling the truth is real. It may cost you relationships, family gatherings, and the comfortable illusion of peace. But the cost of not telling the truth — of staying in the slow motion suicide of protecting a family myth — is your freedom. Your healing. Yourself. You can revise the story. Sometimes you need to do it with fierceness and dedication. But you can do it.
