Episode 6

The Suitcase You've Been Carrying Since Childhood

There's a common assumption that once you've survived childhood and built an adult life — a career, a family, some version of stability — the past no longer needs examining. Midlife tends to prove that assumption wrong.

It's Never Too Late to Look Back

By midlife, most people have finally accumulated something they didn't have earlier: the perspective and stability to actually look at what happened to them as children without being consumed by it. Digging up the past at this stage isn't regression — it's often the first time a person has the emotional resources to process it properly. The years behind someone are not a reason to leave the suitcase unopened; they're what finally make it safe to open.

Waiting until it feels "convenient" to deal with old wounds usually means waiting forever. There's rarely a perfect moment. There's only the decision to finally do it.

The Nervous System Remembers Everything

Childhood experience doesn't just live in memory — it becomes wired into the nervous system itself, shaping how a person communicates, reacts, and relates long after the original events are over. A short temper, a fear of abandonment, a tendency to shut down during conflict — these aren't random personality traits. They're often direct echoes of survival strategies built decades earlier, still running in the background without anyone realizing it.

Recognizing that connection doesn't happen through willpower alone. It requires actually tracing current patterns back to where they were first learned, which is uncomfortable, slow work — but it's the only way those patterns stop running the show unconsciously.

Seeking What Was Never Given

Adults often walk into relationships expecting a partner to provide something a parent never did — validation, safety, unconditional affirmation. When a partner inevitably falls short of that expectation, the disappointment can feel disproportionate to what actually happened between two adults, because it's tangled up with a much older, unmet need.

Even people who spend their careers helping others heal are not immune to this pattern. Recognizing it in yourself, rather than only diagnosing it in others, is often the more uncomfortable and more necessary work.

Bottom Line

There's a little kid still tucked away in a mental closet, carrying wounds nobody ever told them weren't their fault. Going back for that kid isn't weakness, and it isn't too late. It's the work that finally lets a person stop reacting to their past and start actually living apart from it.
